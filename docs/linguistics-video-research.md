# Linguistics for Students — Research Brief & Video Plan

A research pack for making a meaningful educational video that introduces
students (roughly ages 13–20) to **linguistics: the scientific study of
human language**.

---

## 1. The core message (the "one idea" of the video)

> Every human being is born with an extraordinary ability: to learn a
> language without being taught. Linguistics is the science that asks
> *how* language works, *why* all languages share deep patterns, and
> *what* it tells us about the human mind and society.

Key myth to break early: **linguistics is not "knowing many languages"**
(that is a *polyglot*). A linguist studies *how* language works — like a
biologist studies life rather than owning many pets.

---

## 2. The building blocks of language (core branches)

Teach these as a ladder from smallest to largest unit. Each comes with a
hook example that works well on screen.

| Level | Branch | What it studies | Hook example for the video |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sounds (physical) | **Phonetics** | How speech sounds are made, carried and heard | Put your hand on your throat: say "sss" then "zzz". The buzz is your vocal cords — the only difference between *s* and *z*. |
| Sounds (system) | **Phonology** | Which sounds a language treats as different, and sound rules | English treats *p* and *pʰ* (puff of air) as the same sound; Hindi treats them as different: *पल* (pal, moment) vs *फल* (phal, fruit). Hold a paper in front of your mouth to see it. |
| Words | **Morphology** | How words are built from meaningful pieces (morphemes) | *un-believ-able*: 3 pieces, 1 word. Turkish/Tamil can pack a whole English sentence into one long word. |
| Sentences | **Syntax** | How words combine into structures | Chomsky's *"Colorless green ideas sleep furiously"* — perfect grammar, zero sense. Proves grammar is separate from meaning. |
| Meaning | **Semantics** | Literal meaning of words and sentences | "I saw the man with the telescope" — who has the telescope? (ambiguity) |
| Meaning in context | **Pragmatics** | What speakers *intend* beyond the literal words | "Can you pass the salt?" — nobody answers "Yes, I can." |

### Wider fields (for a "where does linguistics go?" segment)

- **Sociolinguistics** — language & society: accents, dialects, code-switching
  (Hinglish: "Main kal college nahi aa raha, I have a test.").
- **Psycholinguistics** — how the brain produces and understands language;
  slips of the tongue, tip-of-the-tongue feeling.
- **Neurolinguistics** — brain areas: Broca's area (production) and
  Wernicke's area (comprehension); aphasia.
- **Historical linguistics** — how languages change and are related.
- **Language acquisition** — how children learn language.
- **Computational linguistics / NLP** — speech recognition, machine
  translation, chatbots and large language models.
- **Forensic linguistics** — language as evidence (authorship, threat letters).
- **Language documentation** — recording endangered languages.

## 3. "Wow" facts and stories (verified, safe to use)

1. **How many languages?** Around **7,100–7,200 living languages** are
   spoken today (Ethnologue, 2025 edition ≈ 7,159). Counts vary because
   the line between "language" and "dialect" is partly political — the
   famous quip: *"A language is a dialect with an army and a navy."*
   (popularised by Max Weinreich).
2. **Endangerment:** About **40–44%** of the world's languages are
   endangered; many have fewer than 1,000 speakers. When the last speaker
   dies, a unique way of seeing the world disappears with them.
3. **Language hotspots:** Papua New Guinea alone has ~840 languages;
   Indonesia ~700; Nigeria ~500.
4. **India's diversity:** The Constitution's 8th Schedule lists **22**
   scheduled languages; the Census 2011 grouped mother tongues into
   **121 languages**; the People's Linguistic Survey of India (G. N. Devy)
   documented about **780 languages**. India hosts four major families:
   Indo-Aryan, Dravidian, Austroasiatic (e.g. Santali, Mundari) and
   Tibeto-Burman — plus isolates like **Nihali**.
5. **Panini (c. 4th century BCE)** wrote the *Aṣṭādhyāyī*, ~4,000 compact
   rules describing Sanskrit grammar — one of the earliest and most
   rigorous linguistic works in history. Modern linguists and computer
   scientists still admire its rule-based, almost algorithmic design.
6. **Language families:** Hindi, English, Persian, Russian and Spanish are
   all distant cousins (Indo-European). Compare *three*: English **three**,
   Hindi **तीन** (*tīn*), Sanskrit **त्रि** (*tri*), Latin **trēs**,
   Russian **три** (*tri*). Sir William Jones noticed this in 1786.
7. **Babies are universal listeners:** Infants can distinguish sounds from
   all languages but narrow down to their native sounds by around
   10–12 months (Janet Werker's research).
8. **Children invent grammar:** Deaf children in Nicaragua (1980s)
   created a brand-new sign language — *Idioma de Señas de Nicaragua* —
   from scratch, with full grammar.
9. **Sign languages are real languages** with their own grammar — e.g.
   Indian Sign Language (ISL) is not "Hindi with hands".
10. **Overregularisation:** Kids say "goed" and "mouses" — words they
    never heard. Proof they learn *rules*, not just copy.
11. **Whistled languages:** Silbo Gomero (Canary Islands) and whistled
    Turkish in Kuşköy convert speech into whistles heard kilometres away.
12. **Sapir–Whorf / linguistic relativity:** The *weak* version is well
    supported — e.g. Guugu Yimithirr speakers (Australia) use compass
    directions ("the ant on your north leg") instead of left/right, and
    are remarkably good at orientation. The *strong* version ("language
    limits thought") is not accepted.
13. **Language is always changing:** "Nice" once meant *foolish*; "awful"
    meant *full of awe*. Slang today is tomorrow's dictionary word.

## 4. Big ideas students should leave with

1. **Descriptive, not prescriptive.** Linguists *describe* how people
   actually speak; they don't judge dialects as "wrong". Every dialect has
   consistent rules. (Great segment against accent-shaming.)
2. **Arbitrariness** (Saussure): there's no natural reason a dog is
   called *dog*, *kutta*, *chien* or *perro*.
3. **Creativity / infinity:** with finite words and rules you can produce
   sentences no one has ever said before — like this one.
4. **Universal Grammar debate:** Chomsky argued humans have an innate
   language faculty; others (usage-based linguists like Tomasello) argue
   language emerges from general learning and social interaction. Present
   both — it's a live scientific question.
5. **Language = identity.** How we speak signals where we're from and who
   we belong to.

## 6. Suggested video structure (~8–10 minutes)

| Time | Segment | Content & visuals |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00–0:40 | **Hook** | Quick montage: a baby babbling, someone code-switching, a voice assistant answering. VO: *"You did something impossible before age 5 — and nobody taught you how."* |
| 0:40–1:30 | **What is linguistics?** | Define it; bust the "polyglot" myth; on-screen "linguist ≠ translator". |
| 1:30–4:30 | **The ladder of language** | Animate the 6 levels (sound → word → sentence → meaning → context). One interactive demo per level: hand-on-throat, paper-puff *pal/phal*, *un-believ-able*, *colorless green ideas*, telescope ambiguity, "pass the salt". |
| 4:30–5:45 | **Languages are family** | Animated family tree; the word *three* across languages; Panini & Sir William Jones. |
| 5:45–6:45 | **How kids learn language** | Babies as universal listeners; "goed/mouses"; Nicaraguan Sign Language story. |
| 6:45–7:45 | **Language, mind & society** | Guugu Yimithirr directions; Hinglish code-switching; "no dialect is wrong". |
| 7:45–8:30 | **Disappearing voices** | ~7,000 languages, ~40% endangered; a map of India's diversity. Emotional beat. |
| 8:30–9:30 | **Why it matters for you** | AI & careers; call to action. |
| 9:30–10:00 | **Closing challenge** | *"Ask your grandparents for one word that doesn't exist in English. Share it in the comments."* |

### Sample opening narration

> "Right now, as you listen to me, your brain is doing something
> no computer could do perfectly until very recently. It's taking a
> stream of noise — vibrations in the air — and turning it into
> meaning. Instantly. Effortlessly. You learned to do this as a baby,
> before you could tie your shoes, and no one gave you a rulebook.
> The science that studies this superpower is called **linguistics**."

### Sample closing narration

> "Every language is a different answer to the same question: how do
> humans share what's in their minds? Seven thousand answers — and half
> of them may vanish this century. Linguistics helps us understand them,
> protect them, and build the technology of tomorrow. So the next time
> you speak, remember: you're using the most complex tool humans have
> ever created."

## 7. Classroom activities to pair with the video

1. **Minimal-pair hunt:** find word pairs differing by one sound
   (*pal/phal*, *kal/khal*, *bat/pat*).
2. **Morpheme puzzle:** break *unhappiness*, *rewriting*, *अनपढ़ता*
   into pieces and give each a meaning.
3. **Invent a mini-language:** 10 words + 3 grammar rules; translate a
   simple sentence.
4. **Dialect map:** list how classmates say "What are you doing?" at home.
5. **Ambiguity collection:** newspaper headlines with two meanings.
6. **Interview an elder:** record 5 words/proverbs from a home language.

## 8. Accuracy notes (avoid common mistakes in the video)

- Don't claim "Inuit have 100 words for snow" — it's an exaggerated myth
  (depends on how you count words in polysynthetic languages).
- Don't say Sanskrit is the "mother of all languages" — it's an ancient
  member of the Indo-European family; Tamil and other Dravidian languages
  are a separate family, and English isn't descended from Sanskrit (they
  share a common ancestor, Proto-Indo-European).
- Don't present the strong Sapir–Whorf hypothesis as fact.
- Language counts are estimates — say "about 7,000".
- Ethnologue's exact figures change yearly — check ethnologue.com before
  publishing.
